# Post-quantum accounts

A Tempo account can change the keys behind its address, so a switch to quantum-safe signatures doesn't mean moving funds.

Crypto accounts today are protected by signatures that a large enough quantum computer could forge, including the secp256k1 keys in Ethereum wallets and the P-256 keys behind passkeys. On most chains, replacing those keys means moving every account to a new address. A [configurable Tempo account](/docs/development/configurable-accounts) keeps its address while its keys change, so when quantum-safe signatures are available, the account switches keys and keeps its address, balances, and integrations.

## What a new key costs on most chains

Take a payroll company that holds stablecoins at one address. Its bank, its exchange, and hundreds of contractors have that address on allowlists and in their records, and its payment providers hold token approvals from it.

On Ethereum, an address is computed from its public key, so a new key is a new address. To move to a quantum-safe key, the company would create a new address, move every balance to it, grant every token approval again, and ask each counterparty to update its allowlists and records. Every account on the network would have to do the same, and all of it before quantum computers can break the old keys.

## How a Tempo account keeps its address

A configurable account's address is computed from the account's first configuration, not from any one key ([configurable account address](/docs/development/configurable-accounts#configurable-account-address)). Its signers are a list the account can change through configuration updates, and the address stays the same through every change.

A [single-key Tempo account](/docs/development/single-key-accounts#single-key-address-derivation) works like an Ethereum account: its key fixes its address. It can [upgrade in place](/docs/accounts/key-management/account-upgrade) to a configurable account at the same address.

## Switching the payroll company's keys

Here's how the payroll company would move to quantum-safe keys on Tempo:

1. **It upgrades its account in place.** The company's original key signs one upgrade, and the account becomes a configurable account at the same address. From then on, the original key approves only if the company lists it as a signer.
2. **It adds a quantum-safe signer.** Once Tempo supports a quantum-safe key type, the company [adds a signer](/docs/accounts/key-management/add-signers) with that key type.
3. **It removes the old keys in the same update.** The old keys approve that one update, which adds the new signer and [removes the old ones](/docs/accounts/key-management/remove-signers) together, so there's no point afterward when an old key still controls the account. The address, balances, and token approvals don't change, and the company's counterparties don't have to do anything.
4. **It replaces its backend's keys.** The company's payroll backend pays with an [access key](/docs/accounts/access-keys) that has its own limits and expiry. The company revokes it and authorizes a replacement without touching the account's signers.

Steps 1, 3, and 4 use features Tempo accounts already have. Step 2 needs a quantum-safe key type, which Tempo doesn't support yet: Tempo accounts accept secp256k1, P-256, and passkey signatures today, and a large enough quantum computer could break all three. Adding a quantum-safe key type is a separate protocol change.

## Why the switch has to happen early

Changing keys only protects an account while its old keys are still safe. Once an attacker can work out a private key from its public key, the attacker and the real owner look the same to Tempo, and nothing can tell them apart. So the company needs to retire its old keys before they can be broken, not after.

A retired key can also still act outside the account's own rules:

- **On other chains.** A secp256k1 key controls the same address on other EVM chains, whatever happens to it on Tempo.
- **In contracts that check signatures themselves.** Contracts such as Permit2 verify signatures on their own, so they may still accept the original key ([what an upgrade changes](/docs/accounts/key-management/account-upgrade#what-an-upgrade-changes)).
